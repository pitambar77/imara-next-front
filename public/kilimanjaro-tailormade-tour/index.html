Best Time To Climb Kilimanjaro

Kilimanjaro can be climbed year-round, but two dry windows offer the clearest skies and highest summit success rates.

Peak Dry Season

Late June to October

The most popular Kilimanjaro climbing season. Cold but stable weather, minimal rainfall, and the clearest summit-night skies of the year. Trails are busier, especially in August.

RainfallLow
CrowdsHigher
VisibilityExcellent
Secondary Dry Season

January to Mid-March

Warmer than the June–October window, with a slightly higher chance of snow near the summit. Many climbers prefer this season for quieter trails and dramatic scenery.

RainfallLow
CrowdsModerate
VisibilityVery Good
Shoulder Months

April–May & November

The long and short rains. Trails are quieter and rates are lower, but expect muddy lower slopes and reduced summit visibility on some days.

RainfallHigh
CrowdsLow

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions every climber asks before booking.

The two dry seasons — late June through October, and January through mid-March — offer the most stable weather and highest summit success rates. The June–October window is colder but has the clearest summit-night skies; the January–March window is warmer with a slightly higher chance of snow near the top. Shoulder months (April–May, November) bring rain but quieter trails and lower rates, for climbers who don't mind the trade-off.

Between 6 and 9 days, depending on route. Extra days on the mountain mean more time to acclimatize, which directly improves your summit success rate — this is why 5–6 day routes like Marangu succeed less often than 7–9 day routes like Lemosho or Northern Circuit. We generally recommend against anything shorter than 6 days unless you have prior high-altitude experience.

Northern Circuit (95–98%) and Lemosho (90–96%) post the highest success rates because their longer itineraries allow more gradual acclimatization. Marangu, the shortest and cheapest route, has the lowest success rate (80–85%) for the same reason. Route choice should balance your available time, budget, and fitness level, not price alone.

Yes. Kilimanjaro is a non-technical trek — no ropes, ice axes, or climbing experience required. Success depends far more on pacing, hydration, and acclimatization than on athletic ability. That said, beginners are usually better served by longer routes (Lemosho, Northern Circuit, Rongai) that give the body more time to adjust to altitude.

Focus on cardiovascular endurance (hiking, running, cycling), leg strength (stairs, squats, lunges), and back-to-back long hikes to build stamina for consecutive trekking days. Training with a loaded daypack for 4–6 hours at a time in the months before your climb is the single best preparation.

Essentials include: layered clothing (base, insulating, waterproof shell), a warm sleeping bag rated to at least -15°C, sturdy broken-in hiking boots, trekking poles, headlamp, and cold-weather gloves/hat for summit night.

Private climbs let you set your own pace, dates, and itinerary, usually at a higher per-person cost. Group climbs join you with other climbers on a fixed departure date, which lowers the price.
